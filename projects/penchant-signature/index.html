Penchant Signature 5 min read

Cutting a bespoke bag order from two weeks of messages to ten minutes

Role

Strategy, UI, frontend and backend

Client

Penchant Signature

Platform

Responsive web

Focus

Order specification and fulfilment

Timeline

April to June 2026

TL;DR

Background

Penchant Signature is a Lagos atelier that has made leather and Aso-Oke bags by hand since 2015. Fourteen people work out of a Lekki workshop, and every piece is made to order across retail, custom, wholesale and private label.

Problem

Orders were specified entirely over messages. Pinning down a single order took roughly two weeks, against a make time of days.

Approach

I designed and built a site that captures the full specification up front, curates it into one brief, and sends that brief to the atelier over WhatsApp. Three months, working alone across strategy, UI, frontend and backend.

Outcome

In beta testing with four testers, a complete order was specified in under ten minutes. The site is built and tested but not yet deployed, so this is a tested result rather than a live one.

The problem

The order was the bottleneck, not the bag

Production was fast. Getting to production was not.

Penchant Signature makes to order, so every piece needs a specification before a single cut is made. That specification was assembled over messages. Leather or Aso-Oke, dimensions, hardware, monogram, delivery - each one a separate exchange, each exchange waiting on whoever in the atelier was free to answer.

The workshop could finish a bag in days. Roughly two weeks went into deciding what the bag was.

The approach

Move the questions to the front

Every question the atelier used to ask by message, asked once, in order, on the site.

The fix was not to remove WhatsApp. The atelier's customers already lived there and the relationship worked. The fix was to stop WhatsApp doing a job it is bad at - holding a long, precise, multi-part specification across days of interruption.

So the site takes the specification and WhatsApp completes the order. A client configures the piece on site, the system curates that into a single brief, and the brief lands in the atelier's thread as one message instead of forty.

Finish the order in WhatsApp, not in checkout

The obvious build was an on-platform checkout. I chose against it. Every existing customer relationship already ran through WhatsApp, and a made-to-order piece needs a human check before anyone cuts leather. A checkout would have replaced a working relationship with a transaction, and forced a payment commitment before the atelier had confirmed it could make the thing.

Payment stays off the platform

A made-to-order piece has no fixed price until the specification is settled, so a checkout would have to quote before the atelier could cost the work. Payment is agreed in the same thread where the brief lands, after the atelier confirms it can make the piece. The site's job ends at a complete brief.

What I built

Seventy screens, one order path

The whole product is one flow, and everything else exists to feed it.

A client lands on the site and selects make to order. From there they input every parameter specific to the piece they want made. The system curates those inputs into a single brief and sends it to the number registered to the platform, where the atelier runs final checks. Once those clear, production begins, and the piece ships on the 5 to 7 working day window the workshop already worked to.

The point of the design is that the specification arrives complete. The atelier still confirms before cutting, but it confirms against a finished brief rather than assembling one question at a time.

I built this independently across three months - strategy, interface, frontend and backend - at a little over seventy screens.

Choose the piece

Entry into the flow from anywhere on the site. The make to order call to action is the only path that matters, so it stays present rather than buried in a menu.

Specify every parameter

Material, dimensions, hardware, monogram, delivery. The same questions the atelier used to ask one at a time, asked once and in order, with nothing left implicit.

Review the curated brief

Before anything sends, the client sees the whole specification as the atelier will receive it. Errors get caught by the person who knows what they meant.

One message, not forty

The brief lands in the atelier's thread as a single message. Final checks happen against a finished document rather than a conversation.

Testing

Placed an order, followed it to the door

I ran the whole process, not just the screens.

I tested end to end with four beta testers including myself, following an order from placement through production to delivery rather than stopping at submission.

The break was in the WhatsApp API handoff - the point where the site passes a completed brief into the thread the atelier actually works from. Given that handoff is the whole premise of the flow, a failure there is not a bug at the edge. It is the product not working. I rebuilt the integration until it held.

Where it stands

Built, tested, waiting on launch

Design and build are done. Documentation is the last thing between this and deploy.

The site is complete and has been tested end to end. What remains is final documentation before handover and launch.

The ten minute figure comes from four testers, not from live orders, and it stays labelled that way until real customers run through it. Once it deploys, the numbers worth watching are how many briefs clear final checks without a follow-up question, and whether the two week specification window actually closes in practice.
